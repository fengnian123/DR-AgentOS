\begin{abstract}
Long-lived embodied agents rarely act on a fresh snapshot: objects may move
outside their view, so a correctly retrieved memory can describe a world that
no longer exists. This problem persists during execution---a prediction that is
correct when a task begins may be stale when the robot arrives. We study
navigation from partial, temporally stale histories, where the agent must infer
the latent current state, act under uncertainty, and revise that inference as
time and evidence accumulate. Our key insight is that memory should provide
temporal evidence about the present rather than be treated as the present. We
introduce \evolvingworldnav{}, which converts timestamped 3D entity histories
into a structured persistence--relocation belief and carries that uncertainty
through action. Within a frozen, zero-shot vision--language control loop, an
event-driven filter propagates beliefs to candidate-specific arrival times,
incorporates only newly informative RGB-D evidence, and replans while allowing
previously inspected states to become plausible again. We further introduce
\pfdbench{}, a human-trace-grounded benchmark with \textbf{54} evolving scenes
and \textbf{803.68k} tasks for controlled evaluation of hidden world evolution.
Across its navigation suite, \evolvingworldnav{} attains \textbf{61.32\%}
First-Inspection SR and \textbf{86.18\%} Search SR, exceeding the strongest
evaluated baseline by \textbf{15.99} and \textbf{14.24} points. Paired controls
show that the advantage is largest under learnable temporal structure, while
ablations isolate the value of maintaining uncertainty and updating it from
visibility-aware evidence. These results position predictive belief revision,
rather than static recall, as a foundation for persistent embodied autonomy.
\end{abstract}
