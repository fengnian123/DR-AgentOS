\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Spatial maps and object memories help long-lived agents reuse observations
across repeated tasks in familiar environments
\citep{gu2024conceptgraphs,liu2024dynamem}. However, those observations
may not describe the world when the agent acts. Human activity changes
task-relevant states beyond the field of view of the robot: a medication box
observed on a bedside table may be returned to a cabinet before the next
request. Further changes can occur while the robot travels, invalidating a
prediction made at the start
of navigation. The agent must therefore plan from observations acquired minutes,
hours, or days earlier and estimate what will be true when it inspects a target.

Under partial observability, the latent world state may change between
observations, making memory stale. Even accurate retrieval from a
geometrically correct map can direct the
agent to an outdated location. Searching from scratch discards useful
history. A persistent agent instead needs a time-indexed predictive belief
that uses past observations to represent uncertainty about unobserved changes
and supports revision during interaction. Predictions must refer to the time
of action, which may differ from the query time.

Memory systems retain object-centric observations and spatial relations, while
predictive representations use temporal histories and recurring activities to
estimate unobserved states
\citep{patel2023routine,saavedraruiz2026predictivegraphs}. PredictiveGraphs, in
particular, exposes Top-$k$ navigation candidates and incorporates observations
collected during active navigation \citep{saavedraruiz2026predictivegraphs}.
As the world continues to evolve, candidate destinations require different
travel times, so the agent must compare their probabilities
at their respective arrival times. Actions can reveal evidence, while the time
spent acting permits further hidden changes. This setting calls for a
closed-loop mechanism that propagates, tests, and revises multiple hypotheses
as actions and world evolution unfold together.

A reliable loop must retain timestamped histories, separate persistence from
relocation, and reserve probability mass outside the known candidate set.
Negative evidence must depend on visibility: clear views of empty locations
weaken the corresponding hypotheses, whereas occluded views remain
inconclusive. Previously inspected locations can become plausible again after
hidden changes. Under finite travel and time budgets, the agent must select
both destinations and informative inspections.

We formalize this problem as \textbf{Evolving-World Navigation}: locating and
visually verifying task-relevant entities from intermittent histories while
their latent states may change unobserved before a query or during execution.
We study portable-object navigation, where external activities cause
relocations that affect task success and often go unobserved. The distinction
between historical observations and current belief also applies to other
changes in scene state.
Given a target, a causal observation history, candidate states, and an action
budget, the agent must infer where the target will be when inspected, gather
physical evidence, and revise its plan without access to hidden transition
events or current ground truth. Fixed-target episodes test query-time inference,
belief-guided search, and evidence-based recovery; episodes with continued
world evolution test arrival-time prediction and replanning. Both settings
provide online observations only along the executed trajectory.

We propose \textbf{\evolvingworldnav{}} (\cref{fig:evolvingworldnav_overview}), a
predictive 4D belief agent. A frozen, zero-shot vision--language
controller invokes memory, prediction, inspection, exploration, and navigation
tools. Within the agent, \pfdnav{} encodes irregular timestamped histories and
factorizes belief into persistence at the last observed state, relocation among
alternative states, and residual mass outside the known set. This factorization
preserves multiple location hypotheses. The underlying 4D memory retains
entity identity, spatial context, observation time, and evidence provenance.

An event-driven predict--observe--replan filter couples action and inference.
The agent propagates its current posterior to each estimated candidate arrival
time and selects a destination based on occupancy probability, expected new
coverage, and travel cost. It then
executes a short action chunk and uses opportunistic or deliberate RGB-D
evidence to update the current-time belief. Clear negative evidence can redirect
the route before the planned destination is fully inspected; inconclusive views
preserve probability for later testing. Time-valid evidence rounds limit
repeated counting of correlated views, while dynamic propagation can
reopen a previously inspected state. Prediction therefore supplies a prior for
action, and embodied interaction continually checks and corrects it.

We introduce \textbf{\evolvingworldbench{}}, a human-trace-grounded simulation
benchmark with 54 evolving scenes and 803.68k tasks spanning state prediction,
object navigation, vision-and-language navigation, and embodied question
answering. The benchmark evaluates inference and action from stale histories
under hidden transitions, including the physical cost of testing uncertain
states. It relates the quality of a time-indexed belief to first inspection,
recovery, path efficiency, and online replanning. Paired static, routine, and
random worlds hold scene and query factors fixed while varying temporal
structure, testing whether gains arise from learnable historical regularity
rather than location frequency, geometry, or additional exploration.

Across the full navigation suite,
\textbf{\evolvingworldnav{}} reaches 61.32\% First-Inspection SR and 86.18\% Search SR,
exceeding DynaMem by 15.99 and 14.24 percentage points, respectively
(\cref{tab:crossbench}). On episodes with execution-time target motion, it
improves Dynamic SR by 18.4 percentage points over
PredictiveGraphs (\cref{tab:n4-online}). Component ablations show that the gain
depends on preserving alternative hypotheses and revising them from online
evidence, rather than only selecting a stronger first destination. Paired
controls show larger gains under learnable routines than under matched random
motion. Evaluations across benchmarks, held-out scenes, frozen VLMs, and
physical robots test transfer beyond a single simulator and controller.
These results suggest that persistent embodied memory should support calibrated
inference about the present and evidence-seeking action in addition to faithful
historical storage.

We make four contributions:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=0.6mm,topsep=1mm]
    \item \textbf{Evolving-World Navigation:}
    We formulate navigation under hidden world evolution, where states may change without being observed, including during execution.

    \item \textbf{Predictive 4D Belief Agent:}
    We introduce \textbf{\evolvingworldnav{}}, coupling structured state prediction with an event-driven predict--observe--replan filter.

    \item \textbf{EvolvingWorld-Bench:}
    We develop a benchmark with 54 evolving scenes and 803.68k tasks for controlled evaluation of predictive navigation.

    \item \textbf{Experimental Evaluation:}
    Simulation and real-robot experiments show consistent gains across environments, benchmarks, and frozen VLMs.
\end{itemize}
