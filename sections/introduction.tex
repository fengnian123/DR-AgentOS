\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Robots deployed for extended periods do not act on snapshots of the world.
Human activity continually changes task-relevant state, often outside the
robot's view. A medication box remembered on a bedside table may have returned
to a cabinet before a request arrives; even a correct query-time prediction may
become stale while the robot is traveling. The robot therefore acts not on the
world itself, but on an internal state assembled from partial observations made
minutes, hours, or days earlier. Long-term autonomy requires distinguishing
\emph{what was observed} from \emph{what is likely true when action reaches it}.

Persistent memories and open-vocabulary maps retain object-centric experience
\citep{gu2024conceptgraphs,liu2024dynamem}; predictive representations infer
unobserved states from temporal regularities
\citep{patel2023routine,saavedraruiz2026predictivegraphs}. PredictiveGraphs also
provides Top-$k$ candidates and incorporates observations collected during
navigation \citep{saavedraruiz2026predictivegraphs}. These are important
ingredients, yet a distinct decision problem remains when action duration
advances the hidden world. Candidate locations have different arrival times;
each action delays the next observation; and the resulting evidence must feed
back into prediction without being counted twice. The missing capability is
thus not prediction or online feedback alone, but closed-loop navigation that
continuously predicts, tests, and revises multiple hypotheses as the world
evolves.

This view yields concrete requirements. Memory must preserve timestamped
history rather than overwrite it with a nominal current map. The agent must
separate whether the last state still holds from where the object may have
moved, retain alternatives instead of committing to a single destination, and
forecast each alternative at its expected inspection time. Evidence must also
be interpreted conditionally: a clear empty view should suppress a hypothesis,
whereas occlusion should preserve uncertainty. Finally, an inspected location
cannot be excluded forever because later hidden evolution may make it plausible
again. Actions therefore serve both navigation and active state estimation.

We formalize this problem as \emph{Evolving-World Navigation}: locating and
visually verifying task-relevant entities from intermittent histories while
their latent states may change outside observation, both before a query and
during execution. To isolate complementary capabilities, we design one setting
that fixes the target after the query and tests query-time inference,
belief-guided search, and evidence-based recovery, and another that continues
the world clock during navigation to test arrival-time prediction and
replanning. Neither setting reveals hidden transitions; only causal observations
along the executed trajectory may update the agent.

We instantiate this formulation with \evolvingworldnav{}
(\cref{fig:evolvingworldnav_overview}). A frozen, zero-shot vision--language
controller invokes memory, prediction, inspection, exploration, and navigation
tools. Its learned \pfdnav{} module encodes irregular, timestamped 3D entity
histories and factorizes belief into persistence at the last observed state,
relocation among alternatives, and residual mass outside the known set. An
event-driven predict--observe--replan filter then propagates this posterior to
candidate-specific arrival times, executes short action chunks, updates from
new visibility-aware RGB-D evidence, and replans. Time-valid evidence rounds
avoid repeated use of correlated frames while allowing previously inspected
states to reopen after further evolution.

Existing benchmarks do not jointly expose stale histories, hidden transitions,
and the physical cost of testing uncertain states. We therefore introduce
\pfdbench{}, a human-trace-grounded simulation benchmark with 54 evolving scenes
and 803.68k tasks spanning state prediction, object navigation,
vision-and-language navigation, and embodied question answering. Its paired
static, routine, and random worlds hold scene and query factors fixed while
varying temporal structure, separating predictive reasoning from location
frequency or spatial convenience.

Experiments connect prediction quality to embodied outcomes. Across the full
navigation suite, \evolvingworldnav{} reaches 61.32\% First-Inspection SR and
86.18\% Search SR, exceeding DynaMem by 15.99 and 14.24 points
(\cref{tab:crossbench}). On episodes with execution-time target motion, it
improves Dynamic SR by 18.4 points over PredictiveGraphs
(\cref{tab:n4-online}); component ablations isolate the contributions of
structured belief and online evidence revision. Cross-benchmark, frozen-VLM,
held-out-scene, and physical-robot evaluations test whether these gains extend
beyond one simulator. Paired controls show the clearest advantage under
learnable routines rather than matched random motion, supporting the scientific
claim that historical regularity is useful when maintained as revisable belief.

Our contributions are fourfold:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=0.6mm,topsep=1mm]
    \item \textbf{Evolving-World Navigation:}
    We formulate navigation under hidden changes before and during execution.

    \item \textbf{Predictive 4D Belief Agent:}
    \evolvingworldnav{} couples structured prediction with event-driven belief revision.

    \item \textbf{P4D-Bench:}
    We provide 54 evolving scenes and 803.68k controlled evaluation tasks.

    \item \textbf{Comprehensive Validation:}
    Simulation and real-robot studies validate the core claims across settings and VLMs.
\end{itemize}
