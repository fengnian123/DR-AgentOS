\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Long-lived embodied agents navigate familiar environments across repeated
tasks. Spatial maps and object memories preserve observations from earlier
visits \citep{gu2024conceptgraphs,liu2024dynamem}. However, those observations
may not describe the world when the agent acts. Human activity changes
task-relevant states outside the robot's view: a medication box observed on a
bedside table may return to a cabinet before the next request. Further changes
can occur while the robot travels, invalidating a prediction made at the start
of navigation. The agent must therefore plan from observations acquired minutes,
hours, or days earlier and estimate what will be true when it inspects a target.

Partial observability makes the distinction between historical observations
and current state consequential. Observations record past states, whereas the
latent world state may evolve between them. This produces temporally stale
memory: even accurate retrieval from a geometrically correct map can direct the
agent to an outdated location. Searching from scratch discards useful
history. A persistent agent instead needs a time-indexed
predictive belief over the latent state. It uses past observations as
evidence, represents uncertainty about unobserved changes, and supports
revision during interaction. The belief must
remain valid at the time of action, which may differ from the query time.

Memory systems retain object-centric observations and spatial relations, while
predictive representations use temporal histories and recurring activities to
estimate unobserved states
\citep{patel2023routine,saavedraruiz2026predictivegraphs}. PredictiveGraphs, in
particular, exposes Top-$k$ navigation candidates and incorporates observations
collected during active navigation \citep{saavedraruiz2026predictivegraphs}.
During execution, the world can continue to evolve. Candidate destinations
require different travel times, so the agent must compare their probabilities
at their respective arrival times. Actions can reveal evidence, while the time
spent acting permits further hidden changes. This setting calls for a
closed-loop mechanism that propagates, tests, and revises multiple hypotheses
as actions and world evolution unfold together.

A reliable loop must retain timestamped histories, distinguish persistence at
the last observed state from relocation, and preserve probability beyond known
candidates. It must also condition negative evidence on visibility: a clear
view of an empty location weakens the corresponding hypothesis, whereas an
occluded view offers little evidence. A previously inspected location can
become plausible again after further hidden evolution. Navigation therefore
requires the agent to select both a destination and an informative inspection
under finite travel and time budgets.

We formalize this problem as \textbf{Evolving-World Navigation}: locating and
visually verifying task-relevant entities from intermittent histories while
their latent states may change outside observation, both before a query and
during execution. Portable-object navigation provides a measurable
instantiation because relocations are consequential, externally caused, and
often entirely unobserved, while the underlying distinction between historical
observation and current belief applies more broadly to changing scene state.
Given a target, a causal observation history, candidate states, and an action
budget, the agent must infer where the target will be when inspected, gather
physical evidence, and revise its plan without access to hidden transition
events or current ground truth. To isolate complementary capabilities, we
evaluate fixed-target episodes that test query-time inference, belief-guided
search, and evidence-based recovery, and continuing-evolution episodes that
test arrival-time prediction and replanning. Both expose only observations
acquired along the executed trajectory.

We propose \textbf{\evolvingworldnav{}} (\cref{fig:evolvingworldnav_overview}), a
predictive 4D belief agent. A frozen, zero-shot vision--language
controller invokes memory, prediction, inspection, exploration, and navigation
tools. Within the agent, \pfdnav{} encodes irregular timestamped histories and
factorizes belief into persistence at the last observed state, relocation among
alternative states, and residual mass outside the known set. This factorization
preserves distinct persistence and relocation hypotheses without collapsing
uncertainty to one destination. The belief draws on a persistent 4D
memory that retains entity identity, spatial context, observation time, and
evidence provenance.

An event-driven predict--observe--replan filter couples action and inference.
Before selecting a destination, the agent
propagates its current posterior to each candidate's estimated arrival time and
balances occupancy probability, expected new coverage, and travel cost. It then
executes a short action chunk and uses opportunistic or deliberate RGB-D
evidence to update the current-time belief. Clear negative evidence can redirect
the route before the planned destination is fully inspected; inconclusive views
preserve probability for later testing. Time-valid evidence rounds limit
repeated counting of correlated views, while dynamic propagation can
reopen a previously inspected state. Prediction therefore supplies a prior for
action, and embodied interaction continually checks and corrects it.

To evaluate stale histories, hidden transitions, and the physical cost of
testing uncertain states jointly, we introduce \textbf{\evolvingworldbench{}}, a
human-trace-grounded simulation benchmark with 54 evolving scenes and 803.68k
tasks spanning state prediction, object navigation, vision-and-language
navigation, and embodied question answering. It links the quality of a
time-indexed belief to first inspection, recovery, path efficiency, and online
replanning. Paired static, routine, and
random worlds hold scene and query factors fixed while varying temporal
structure, testing whether gains arise from learnable historical regularity
rather than location frequency, geometry, or additional exploration.

Experiments support this interpretation. Across the full navigation suite,
\textbf{\evolvingworldnav{}} improves both initial inspection and eventual
search (\cref{tab:crossbench}). When targets move during execution, it recovers
more reliably while reducing unnecessary travel (\cref{tab:n4-online}).
Component ablations attribute these gains to maintaining alternative
hypotheses and revising them with online evidence. Paired controls show the
clearest advantage under learnable routines, while cross-benchmark,
held-out-scene, frozen-VLM, and physical-robot evaluations support transfer
beyond a single simulator or controller. Together, these findings suggest
that persistent embodied memory should support inference about the current
world and evidence-seeking action alongside faithful historical storage.

Our contributions are fourfold:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=0.6mm,topsep=1mm]
    \item \textbf{Evolving-World Navigation:}
    We formulate navigation under hidden world evolution, where states may change outside the agent's view and during execution.

    \item \textbf{Predictive 4D Belief Agent:}
    We introduce \textbf{\evolvingworldnav{}}, coupling structured state prediction with an event-driven predict--observe--replan filter.

    \item \textbf{EvolvingWorld-Bench:}
    We establish a large-scale benchmark with 54 evolving scenes and 803.68k tasks for controlled evaluation of predictive navigation.

    \item \textbf{Comprehensive Validation:}
    Extensive simulation and real-robot experiments demonstrate consistent gains across environments, benchmarks, and frozen VLMs.
\end{itemize}
