\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Long-lived robots must distinguish what they remember from what is still true,
even as the world changes while they move. A bowl observed in a cabinet
yesterday may be beside the sink when a task starts, then move again while the
robot is en route; both changes can occur outside its view. Accurate retrieval
can therefore select a stale destination, and an initially correct prediction
can become outdated before arrival. Navigation must use accumulated experience
while reasoning about its present validity.

Persistent memories and open-vocabulary maps ground objects and language in
spatial experience
\citep{huang2023vlmaps,gu2024conceptgraphs,liu2024dynamem}. Predictive scene
representations and routine models already learn temporal regularities in
object states and placements
\citep{patel2023routine,kurenkov2023dynamic,saavedraruiz2026predictivegraphs,
argenziano2026flowmaps}, while active search uses semantic relations or beliefs
to guide exploration \citep{zheng2021cos,ginting2024seek}. Our question is how
to connect temporal prediction to physical decisions under hidden world
evolution: retaining alternative locations for recovery and distinguishing
evidence of absence from an inconclusive view.

We study \emph{persistent navigation in evolving worlds} through
portable-object navigation: given a target, a causal history of intermittent
observations, candidate locations, and a finite action budget, the agent must
find the target without access to hidden transitions or current ground truth.
The main protocol holds the target fixed after the query, isolating inference
across the preceding observation gap; an online-dynamic extension permits
further hidden changes during execution. This separates prediction, search,
and recovery from continuous target tracking.

Our central insight is to separate \emph{whether the last observed state still
holds} from \emph{where the object may have moved}, retaining both as
probabilistic hypotheses. We implement this in \evolvingworldnav{}, an embodied
agent with \textbf{4D Predictive Memory}
(\cref{fig:evolvingworldnav_overview}). Persistent 3D entities carry timestamped
state histories and observation provenance. The predictive core, \pfdnav{},
uses a continuous-time Transformer to infer a persistence--relocation belief
from irregular observations. A frozen vision--language controller accesses
the predictor through typed tools and orders inspections by candidate
probability, expected visibility, and travel cost. Visibility-aware Bayesian
updates distinguish an empty location from an obstructed view, revising the
belief before replanning. New observations return to memory, closing the loop
between history, prediction, and action.

We introduce \pfdbench{}, a human-trace-grounded simulation benchmark with
54 scenes and 803.68k task instances across state prediction, object
navigation, vision-and-language navigation, and embodied question answering.
Paired static, routine, and random worlds hold scene and query factors fixed
while varying hidden transitions. Our primary evaluations focus on
current-state prediction and navigation.
\evolvingworldnav{} achieves 61.32\% First-Go SR and 86.18\% Search SR,
exceeding DynaMem, the strongest evaluated baseline on both metrics, by
15.99 and 5.18 percentage points (\cref{tab:crossbench}). Evaluations on
FindingDory, GOAT-Bench, held-out scenes, and physical robots test transfer
beyond the primary benchmark. Paired controls show the clearest gains under
learnable routines; static and random worlds expose limits when temporal
prediction adds little information (\cref{tab:worlds}).

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=5mm,itemsep=0.6mm,topsep=1mm]
    \item \textbf{A controlled navigation setting} separating hidden change
    across observation gaps from further evolution during execution.
    \item \textbf{A predictive-memory agent} coupling persistence--relocation
    beliefs with cost-aware inspection and visibility-aware evidence updates.
    \item \textbf{A human-trace-grounded benchmark} with causal histories and
    paired-world controls to identify when temporal prediction improves
    embodied decisions.
\end{itemize}
