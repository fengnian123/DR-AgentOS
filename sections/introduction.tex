\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Long-lived robots must distinguish what they remember from what is still true.
A bowl observed in a cabinet yesterday may be beside the sink when a task
starts, even though the robot has witnessed no change. Retrieving the earlier
observation correctly can therefore send the robot to the wrong destination.
The bowl may move again while the robot is en route, making even an initially
correct prediction stale before arrival. Searching from scratch discards
accumulated experience, while treating the last observation as current can
waste travel and inspection effort. Persistent navigation requires both
retaining historical evidence and assessing its relevance to the current task.

Spatial memories and open-vocabulary maps ground objects and language in past
experience
\citep{huang2023vlmaps,gu2024conceptgraphs,liu2024dynamem}. Predictive scene
representations and routine models further exploit temporal regularities to
anticipate object states and placements
\citep{patel2023routine,kurenkov2023dynamic,
saavedraruiz2026predictivegraphs,argenziano2026flowmaps}. These advances
establish that history can support prediction and search as well as retrieval.
We focus on how an agent should use an uncertain location prediction to choose
and revise physical inspections. A likely but distant candidate may be less
useful to inspect first than a nearby candidate with a clear view. Committing
to one destination also discards alternatives needed for recovery, while
treating every failed detection as absence overlooks occlusion. A useful belief
must therefore connect temporal inference, inspection cost, and the reliability
of new evidence.

We study \emph{persistent navigation in evolving worlds} through
portable-object navigation. Given a target, observations from intermittent
visits, candidate locations, and a finite action budget, the agent must locate
and visually verify the target without access to hidden transitions or its
current ground-truth state. Only observations available at each decision time
may inform the agent. The main protocol holds the target fixed after the query,
isolating inference across the preceding observation gap. An online-dynamic
extension permits additional hidden transitions during execution. This
separation distinguishes navigation based on stale observations from continuous
target tracking and makes the roles of prediction, search, and recovery explicit.

Our approach separates two questions: \emph{does the object occupy its last
observed location, and if not, where might it be?} We factorize the current-state
belief into a persistence probability and a distribution over alternatives.
Persistence measures agreement with the last observation, including objects
that leave and return. Elapsed time and routine phase can affect these
hypotheses differently: two equally old observations need not imply the same
current location if one query falls before a habitual activity and the other
after it. The resulting belief retains alternatives that can guide recovery
after an incorrect first destination. Physical inspection tests these
hypotheses: a clear view of an empty location reduces its probability, whereas
an obstructed view leaves more uncertainty.

We implement this approach in \evolvingworldnav{}, an embodied agent with
\textbf{4D Predictive Memory} (\cref{fig:evolvingworldnav_overview}). Its
memory associates persistent 3D entities with timestamped state histories and
observation provenance, preserving earlier versions when states change. The
predictive core, \pfdnav{}, encodes irregular observations with a continuous-time
Transformer and estimates the structured belief, including probability mass
for locations outside the known candidate set. A frozen vision--language
controller accesses memory, prediction, and navigation through typed tools.
The agent orders inspections using candidate probability, expected visibility,
and travel cost, then applies visibility-aware Bayesian updates after negative
observations and replans. Newly acquired observations return to memory,
connecting temporal inference with subsequent decisions. In online-dynamic
episodes, predictions are evaluated at estimated arrival times so that the
agent can account for changes during travel.

We introduce \pfdbench{} to evaluate the interaction between history,
prediction, and action. This human-trace-grounded simulation benchmark contains
54 scenes and 803.68k task instances across current-state prediction, object
navigation, vision-and-language navigation, and embodied question answering.
Human traces constrain executable scene histories rather than serve as copied
trajectories. Paired static, routine, and random worlds hold scene and query
factors fixed while varying the hidden transition process. The random control
matches legal candidates and marginal movement statistics while removing
temporal dependencies, testing whether historical regularities improve
decisions beyond location frequency and spatial convenience. Our primary
experiments focus on current-state prediction and navigation. Separate metrics
let us examine how state-estimation quality translates into successful physical
search under a finite action budget.

Under the main fixed-target protocol, \evolvingworldnav{} achieves 61.32\%
First-Go SR and 86.18\% Search SR, exceeding DynaMem, the strongest evaluated
baseline on both metrics, by 15.99 and 5.18 percentage points, respectively
(\cref{tab:crossbench}). Evaluations on FindingDory, GOAT-Bench, held-out
scenes, and physical robots examine performance beyond \pfdbench{}.
The paired controls show a gain over Last Seen in routine worlds, but not in
static or random worlds (\cref{tab:worlds}). These results support predictive
memory for worlds in which object relocations follow learnable routines.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=5mm,itemsep=0.6mm,topsep=1mm]
    \item \textbf{A controlled navigation setting.} We formulate
    portable-object navigation from intermittent histories, with a fixed-target
    protocol and an online-dynamic extension.
    \item \textbf{A structured predictive-memory agent.} We introduce
    \evolvingworldnav{}, coupling a learned persistence--relocation belief
    with cost-aware inspection and visibility-aware evidence updates.
    \item \textbf{A benchmark for hidden world evolution.} We introduce
    \pfdbench{} with causal histories and paired static/routine/random controls
    to evaluate when temporal prediction improves embodied decisions.
\end{itemize}
