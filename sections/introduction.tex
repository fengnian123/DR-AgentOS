\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Robots deployed for extended periods do not act on fresh snapshots of the
world. Human activity continually changes task-relevant state, often outside
the robot's field of view. A medication box remembered on a bedside table may
have been returned to a cabinet before a request arrives; even a prediction
that is correct when the task begins may become stale while the robot is
traveling. The robot therefore acts not on the world itself, but on an internal
state assembled from observations made minutes, hours, or days earlier. For
long-lived embodied agents, the relevant question is no longer only what was
observed or where an entity was, but what is likely to be true when the agent
can physically inspect and act on it.

This exposes a general consequence of partial observability that is easy to
miss in short-horizon benchmarks. An observation is an immutable historical
fact; the current world state is a latent variable that may continue evolving
between observations. Conflating the two produces \textbf{temporally stale
memory}: a map can be geometrically accurate and its retrieval can be correct,
yet the resulting action can still fail because the stored state is no longer
current. Discarding history and searching from scratch avoids this assumption
but wastes accumulated experience. A persistent agent instead needs a
time-indexed \textbf{predictive belief} over the current latent world state---a
distribution that treats past observations as evidence, expresses uncertainty
about unobserved change, and remains revisable through interaction.

Persistent memories and open-vocabulary maps provide an important foundation
by retaining object-centric observations and spatial relations
\citep{gu2024conceptgraphs,liu2024dynamem}. Predictive representations further
use temporal histories and recurring activities to estimate unobserved states
\citep{patel2023routine,saavedraruiz2026predictivegraphs}. PredictiveGraphs, in
particular, exposes Top-$k$ navigation candidates and incorporates observations
collected during active navigation \citep{saavedraruiz2026predictivegraphs}.
These capabilities establish essential ingredients, but a distinct decision
problem remains when the world clock advances during the search itself.
Different hypotheses require different travel times, so their probabilities
must be compared at their respective arrival times. Each action both reveals
new evidence and delays the next observation, during which the hidden state may
change again. The missing capability is therefore not future prediction or
online feedback in isolation, but a closed-loop mechanism that repeatedly
propagates, tests, and revises multiple hypotheses as action and world evolution
unfold together.

Such a mechanism must satisfy several requirements. First, memory should retain
timestamped histories rather than overwrite them with a nominal current map.
Second, prediction should distinguish whether the last observed state remains
valid from where the entity may have moved, while preserving alternatives and
probability outside the known candidate set. Third, evidence must be
visibility-conditioned: a clearly observed empty location provides strong
negative evidence, whereas an occluded view should not eliminate the same
hypothesis. Finally, a location verified as empty at one time cannot be removed
forever, because later hidden evolution may make it plausible again. Navigation
thus becomes active state estimation: the agent chooses not only a destination,
but also which uncertain hypothesis is worth testing under finite time and
travel budgets.

We formalize this problem as \textbf{Evolving-World Navigation}: locating and
visually verifying task-relevant entities from intermittent histories while
their latent states may change outside observation, both before a query and
during execution. Portable-object navigation provides a measurable
instantiation because relocations are consequential, externally caused, and
often entirely unobserved, while the underlying distinction between historical
observation and current belief applies more broadly to changing scene state.
Given a target, a causal observation history, candidate states, and an action
budget, the agent must infer where the target will be when inspected, gather
physical evidence, and revise its plan without access to hidden transition
events or current ground truth. To isolate complementary capabilities, we
design one setting that fixes the target after the query and tests query-time
inference, belief-guided search, and evidence-based recovery, and another that
continues the world clock during navigation to test arrival-time prediction and
replanning. Both expose only observations acquired along the executed
trajectory.

This formulation naturally leads to \evolvingworldnav{}
(\cref{fig:evolvingworldnav_overview}), a predictive 4D belief agent rather
than a stronger retrieval system. A frozen, zero-shot vision--language
controller invokes memory, prediction, inspection, exploration, and navigation
tools. Within the agent, \pfdnav{} encodes irregular timestamped histories and
factorizes belief into persistence at the last observed state, relocation among
alternative states, and residual mass outside the known set. This factorization
captures two different questions---whether the previous fact is still valid
and, if not, what transition is plausible---without prematurely collapsing
uncertainty to one destination. The belief is grounded in a persistent 4D
memory that retains entity identity, spatial context, observation time, and
evidence provenance.

Action and inference are coupled through an event-driven
predict--observe--replan filter. Before selecting a destination, the agent
propagates its current posterior to each candidate's estimated arrival time and
balances occupancy probability, expected new coverage, and travel cost. It then
executes a short action chunk and uses opportunistic or deliberate RGB-D
evidence to update the current-time belief. Clear negative evidence can redirect
the route before the planned destination is fully inspected; inconclusive views
preserve probability for later testing. Time-valid evidence rounds prevent
correlated frames from being counted repeatedly, while dynamic propagation can
reopen a previously inspected state. Prediction therefore supplies a prior for
action, and embodied interaction continually checks and corrects it.

Existing benchmarks do not jointly expose stale histories, hidden transitions,
and the physical cost of testing uncertain states. We introduce \pfdbench{}, a
human-trace-grounded simulation benchmark with 54 evolving scenes and 803.68k
tasks spanning state prediction, object navigation, vision-and-language
navigation, and embodied question answering. Rather than measuring prediction
in isolation, it links the quality of a time-indexed belief to first inspection,
recovery, path efficiency, and online replanning. Paired static, routine, and
random worlds hold scene and query factors fixed while varying temporal
structure, testing whether gains arise from learnable historical regularity
rather than location frequency, geometry, or additional exploration.

Experiments support this interpretation. Across the full navigation suite,
\evolvingworldnav{} reaches 61.32\% First-Inspection SR and 86.18\% Search SR,
exceeding DynaMem by 15.99 and 14.24 points (\cref{tab:crossbench}). On episodes
with execution-time target motion, it improves Dynamic SR by 18.4 points over
PredictiveGraphs (\cref{tab:n4-online}). Component ablations show that the gain
depends on preserving alternative hypotheses and revising them from online
evidence, rather than only selecting a stronger first destination. Paired
controls show the clearest advantage under learnable routines rather than
matched random motion, while cross-benchmark, held-out-scene, frozen-VLM, and
physical-robot evaluations test transfer beyond one simulator and controller.
Together, these results support a broader view of embodied memory: its value is
not only whether it faithfully stores the past, but whether it enables
calibrated reasoning and evidence-seeking action about the present.

Our contributions are fourfold:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=0.6mm,topsep=1mm]
    \item \textbf{Evolving-World Navigation:}
    We formulate navigation under hidden world evolution, where states may change outside the agent's view and during execution.

    \item \textbf{Predictive 4D Belief Agent:}
    We introduce \evolvingworldnav{}, coupling structured state prediction with an event-driven predict--observe--replan filter.

    \item \textbf{P4D-Bench:}
    We establish a large-scale benchmark with 54 evolving scenes and 803.68k tasks for controlled evaluation of predictive navigation.

    \item \textbf{Comprehensive Validation:}
    Extensive simulation and real-robot experiments demonstrate consistent gains across environments, benchmarks, and frozen VLMs.
\end{itemize}
