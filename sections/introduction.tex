\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Long-lived robots must distinguish what they remember from what is still true.
A bowl observed in a cabinet yesterday may be beside the sink when a task starts, even though the robot has witnessed no change. 
Retrieving the earlier observation correctly can therefore send the robot to the wrong destination.
The bowl may move again while the robot is en route, making even an initially
correct prediction stale before arrival. 
Searching from scratch discards
accumulated experience, while treating the last observation as current can
waste travel and inspection effort. 
Persistent navigation requires both retaining historical evidence and assessing its relevance to the current task.

Persistent memories and open-vocabulary maps retain past object observations
\citep{gu2024conceptgraphs,liu2024dynamem}, while predictive scene
representations estimate a query-time state from historical regularities
\citep{patel2023routine,saavedraruiz2026predictivegraphs}. Most existing
methods retrieve past state or estimate state at a decision time.
PredictiveGraphs goes further by exposing Top-$k$ navigation candidates,
online observation updates, and an agent/tool architecture
\citep{saavedraruiz2026predictivegraphs}. The remaining gap is not the mere
presence of prediction or feedback, but a filtering loop for a world that can
continue changing during execution: the posterior must be propagated to each
candidate's arrival time, revised only by newly visible evidence, and fed back
into prediction and replanning without double counting. An occluded view must
not eliminate a hypothesis, while a previously empty location may become
plausible again after further hidden evolution.

Unlike online target tracking, the agent may receive no observation when a
change occurs and must begin acting before any hypothesis can be verified. The
challenge is therefore not only to maintain a current map, but to decide which
uncertain world state is worth testing and how strongly a failed inspection
should alter subsequent action.

We study \emph{persistent navigation in evolving worlds} through
portable-object navigation, namely navigation for movable objects whose
locations may change over time. Given a target, intermittent observations,
candidate locations, and a finite action budget, the agent must locate and
visually verify the target without access to hidden transitions or its current
ground-truth state. \pfdbench{} evaluates two complementary execution settings.
In N1--N3, the target is fixed after the query, isolating query-time state
inference, belief-guided search, and evidence-based recovery without further
target motion. In N4, the world clock continues during execution and additional
hidden transitions may occur while the agent is en route, testing arrival-time
prediction and replanning. Only evidence available at each decision time may
inform the agent; unlike continuous target tracking, neither setting reveals a
transition when it occurs.

Our approach separates two questions: \emph{does the object occupy its last
observed location, and if not, where might it be?} We factorize the current-state
belief into a persistence probability and a distribution over alternatives.
Persistence measures agreement with the last observation, including objects
that leave and return. Elapsed time and routine phase can affect these
hypotheses differently: two equally old observations need not imply the same
current location if one query falls before a habitual activity and the other
after it. The resulting belief retains alternatives that can guide action when
the initial route becomes stale. Opportunistic and deliberate observations test
these hypotheses: a clear view of an empty location reduces its probability,
whereas an obstructed view leaves more uncertainty.

We implement this approach in \evolvingworldnav{}, an embodied agent with
\textbf{4D Predictive Memory} (\cref{fig:evolvingworldnav_overview}). A frozen
vision--language controller invokes memory, prediction, inspection, and
navigation tools, while the learned predictor supplies the current-state
belief. Within \evolvingworldnav{}, \pfdnav{} serves as this predictive module:
it encodes irregular observations with a continuous-time Transformer and
estimates a structured persistence--relocation belief, including probability
mass outside the known candidate set. The memory associates persistent 3D
entities with timestamped state histories and provenance. The agent orders
inspections using arrival-time belief, expected new visibility, and travel
cost. An event-driven filter alternates short navigation chunks, visibility-
aware measurement updates, temporal propagation, and replanning; it can reopen
previously inspected candidates or explore when probability remains outside the
known set.

We introduce \pfdbench{} to evaluate the interaction between history,
prediction, and action. This human-trace-grounded simulation benchmark contains
54 scenes and 803.68k task instances across current-state prediction, object
navigation, vision-and-language navigation, and embodied question answering.
Human traces constrain executable scene histories rather than serve as copied
trajectories. Paired static, routine, and random worlds hold scene and query
factors fixed while varying the hidden transition process. The random control
matches legal candidates and marginal movement statistics while removing
temporal dependencies, testing whether historical regularities improve
decisions beyond location frequency and spatial convenience. Our primary
experiments focus on current-state prediction and navigation. Separate metrics
let us examine how state-estimation quality translates into successful physical
search under a finite action budget.

Across the full N1--N5 navigation suite, \evolvingworldnav{} achieves 61.32\%
First-Inspection SR and 86.18\% Search SR, exceeding DynaMem, the strongest evaluated
baseline on both metrics, by 15.99 and 5.18 percentage points, respectively
(\cref{tab:crossbench}). Evaluations on FindingDory, GOAT-Bench, held-out
scenes, and physical robots examine performance beyond \pfdbench{}.
The paired controls show that the gain over Last Seen is substantially larger
in routine than in matched random worlds (\cref{tab:worlds}). These results
support predictive memory when object relocations contain learnable temporal
structure. Our contributions are fourfold:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=1mm]
    \item \textbf{Evolving-World Navigation:}
    We formulate navigation under hidden world evolution, where states may change outside the agent's view and during execution.

    \item \textbf{Predictive 4D Belief Agent:}
    We introduce \evolvingworldnav{}, coupling structured state prediction with an event-driven predict--observe--replan filter.

    \item \textbf{P4D-Bench:}
    We establish a large-scale benchmark with 54 evolving scenes and 803.68k tasks for controlled evaluation of predictive navigation.

    \item \textbf{Comprehensive Validation:}
    Extensive simulation and real-robot experiments demonstrate consistent gains across environments, benchmarks, and frozen VLMs.
\end{itemize}
