\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

We evaluate three linked questions: whether \pfdnav{} predicts the latent
current state more accurately and with better calibration; whether its belief
improves navigation destinations and search efficiency; and whether the
closed-loop agent recovers when its initial prediction conflicts with physical
evidence. Benchmark experiments follow the native protocols described below;
within each comparison, methods share perception, candidate states, inspection
viewpoints, low-level control, and action budgets.

\subsection{Evaluation Matrix}

\paragraph{Cross-benchmark evaluation.}
We evaluate the complete agent on \pfdbench{}, FindingDory
\citep{yadav2025findingdory}, and GOAT-Bench \citep{khanna2024goatbench}.
The three benchmarks probe complementary capabilities rather than sharing a
single pooled score. \pfdbench{} isolates belief over hidden current state and
its effect on evidence-aware navigation. FindingDory evaluates long-horizon
memory through high-level goal selection and conditional low-level execution;
the compact comparison reports HL-SR and HL-SPL, while the appendix retains the
full high- and low-level breakdown. GOAT-Bench evaluates lifelong
multi-goal navigation; in addition to its official SR and SPL, we report
Repeat-SR on subtasks whose target identity or containing region has appeared
in the agent's prior stream. The latter is an explicitly marked diagnostic of
memory reuse, not an official leaderboard metric. Category, language, and image
goals are disaggregated in the appendix. On \pfdbench{}, First-Go SR, Search SR,
and SPL jointly expose prediction quality, eventual task success, and path
efficiency. Every comparison uses the benchmark's native protocol, and reported
external numbers are separated from reproduced results.

\paragraph{Prediction-only study.}
We train on days 0--29, select configurations on days 30--34, and evaluate on
days 35--39 of five HSSD scene histories (5,475/910/915 queries).
Temporal evaluation pools the five scenes; leave-one-scene-out (LOSO) trains
and selects on four scenes and tests on the fifth. All methods share query
identities and legal candidates. We report temporal \emph{changed} queries
($n=322$), whose current state differs from the last positive observation,
and pooled LOSO \emph{change-balanced} queries ($n=644$), with equal changed
and unchanged counts within observation-age bins. These are conditional
prediction metrics, not navigation success rates. The complete natural
distribution and implementation details appear in
\cref{app:prediction-evidence}. This single-seed study evaluates the full
Ours-Memory configuration after development informed by earlier test results;
its richer inputs and search budget differ from the frozen legacy baselines.
A separate three-seed study with fresh synthetic trajectories and matched
neural inputs tests whether its advantages survive stronger controls.



\paragraph{Cross-domain simulation.}
To distinguish temporal reasoning from renderer- or dataset-specific fitting,
we evaluate one canonical task in each environment: R2R-CE on MP3D,
HM3D-OVON on HM3D, the native PointNav protocol on Habitat-GS, SAGE-Bench on
InteriorGS, and \pfdbench{} on dynamic HSSD scenes. Each column keeps its
official split, goal specification, and success rule; scores are not pooled
across tasks. We hold perception and low-level control fixed within a column
and report either exact-protocol published results or matched runs from
released checkpoints. This design covers standard meshes, Gaussian-splatting
simulators, and evolving worlds without treating heterogeneous tasks as a
single metric.



\subsection{Real-World Evaluation}
\label{sec:real-world}
We further evaluate \evolvingworldnav{} on three commercial legged robotic platforms,
DEEP Robotics LYNX M20, X30, and Lite3, across both indoor and outdoor
environments. M20 serves as the primary platform for quantitative evaluation,
while X30 and Lite3 are used to examine cross-platform transfer. The memory
representation, prediction module, belief update, and high-level search policy
are shared across platforms; only the robot-specific perception and low-level
navigation interfaces differ. Manufacturer-reported platform specifications
are provided in the appendix.

The real-world evaluation focuses on persistent target search under
environmental changes that unfold across multiple observation sessions. In the
indoor setting, both objects and people may change locations over time, with
their histories exhibiting different degrees of temporal and activity-related
regularity. The outdoor setting extends the same formulation to larger and
less constrained spaces, where targets likewise undergo recurring or
irregular location changes. In both settings, the agent receives historical
observations before a query and must infer the target's current likely state,
prioritize candidate locations, navigate to gather physical evidence, and
revise its belief when new observations contradict the initial prediction.

Targets remain stationary within an individual search episode, so the
evaluation isolates hidden changes between observations rather than target
motion during execution. Compared methods receive the same pre-query history,
candidate locations, starting condition, perception interface, and
platform-specific low-level navigation stack. Success requires autonomous
online confirmation of the queried target within the search budget; missed
detections, navigation failures, timeouts, and human interventions count as
failures. We report Search SR, First-Go SR, travel distance, elapsed time,
number of inspected locations, and recovery after an incorrect first
prediction. Detailed task construction, robot specifications, and scoring
procedures are provided in \cref{app:real-world}.


\subsection{Baselines and Ablations}
\label{sec:baselines}

We select baselines from the implementations that have been validated in our
evaluation stack and organize them by the capability they contribute. The
\emph{open-vocabulary and lifelong navigation} group contains VLFM
\citep{yokoyama2024vlfm}, ZSON \citep{majumdar2022zson}, the FindingDory agent
\citep{yadav2025findingdory}, and LagMemo GLUE \citep{zhou2025lagmemo}. These
methods test frontier-based semantic search, learned zero-shot navigation,
long-horizon episodic recall, and persistent 3DGS language memory,
respectively. The \emph{structured spatio-temporal memory} group contains
HOV-SG \citep{werby2024hovsg} and DynaMem \citep{liu2024dynamem}, representing
hierarchical open-vocabulary graphs and online dynamic metric-semantic memory.
The \emph{predictive world-model} group contains SLaTe-PRO
\citep{patel2023routine}, SGM+NEP \citep{kurenkov2023dynamic}, FlowMaps
\citep{argenziano2026flowmaps}, and the Perpetua predictor from
PredictiveGraphs \citep{saavedraruiz2026predictivegraphs}.

Navigation systems are executed through their released policies or benchmark
adapters. Memory-only methods replace \evolvingworldnav{}' memory backend while using
the same detector, goal interface, and low-level controller. Prediction-only
methods receive the same causal history and candidate set; their state scores
are passed to the same inspection planner. This component-aligned adaptation is
reported separately from native published results and prevents a stronger
detector or controller from being mistaken for better temporal reasoning.
Last Seen, Frequency, Markov, and Random Forest remain lightweight diagnostic
baselines in the dedicated \pfdbench{} and predictor tables rather than the
headline cross-benchmark comparison.

For the cross-environment evaluation in \cref{tab:domains}, baselines are
additionally stratified by training regime. MTU3D, SoftNav, NaVid, Uni-NaVid,
NaVILA, and D3D-VLP represent navigation-trained or task-adapted policies
\citep{zhu2025mtu3d,wu2026softnav,zhang2024navid,zhang2024uninavid,
cheng2024navila,wang2026d3dvlp}. VLFM, AO-Planner, TANGO, DRIVE-Nav, and
Uni-LaViRA represent training-free or target-benchmark zero-shot agents
\citep{yokoyama2024vlfm,chen2025aoplanner,ziliotto2025tango,
gao2026drivenav,ding2026unilavira}. This selection spans classical policies and
recent generalist agents while retaining only methods with reported scores on
the selected task or sufficient released code and checkpoints for reproduction.

N4 additionally compares against Transit-Aware Planning under its required
online target-path input \citep{dorbala2026portable}. Oracle Current State
provides the navigation upper bound. Oracle Activity supplies the ground-truth
current activity and is reported only as a diagnostic rather than a fair
baseline.

For EQA, all methods use the same question templates, answer ontology, and
normalization. Queried information must be recovered from the memory available
to each method. When a learned language readout is required, it is held fixed
across memory methods so that the comparison primarily measures memory quality
rather than language-model capacity.

The principal ablations remove continuous-time features; the
persistence--relocation factorization; instance-specific history; calendar
context; negative evidence; visibility calibration; Bayesian belief updating;
or the full distribution in favor of Top-1 prediction. \emph{P4D-Belief Prior}
predicts once and searches without posterior updates, while full \pfdnav{} closes
the observe--update--replan loop.

We additionally replace the predictive component with Frequency, Markov,
Random Forest, GRU, direct Transformer, and the structured continuous-time
Transformer while keeping memory, candidates, and the agent policy fixed. To
test whether the system depends on one proprietary controller, we run the same
typed memory and navigation tools with GPT-family and Qwen-VL-family foundation
models, plus a third VLM where available. Comparisons require fixed model versions, prompts, temperature, tool schemas,
and inference budgets; these configuration fields belong to the reproducibility
record in \cref{app:implementation}.

\subsection{Metrics and Reporting}
\label{sec:metrics}

We first assess predictive belief independently of navigation using state
Top-1/Top-$k$ accuracy, negative log-likelihood (NLL), Brier score, expected
calibration error (ECE), true-state rank, mean reciprocal rank (MRR), and
persistence AUROC.

For navigation, we report Success Rate (SR), SPL, path length, completion time,
First-Go SR, number of inspected candidates, and search cost. N3 additionally
reports Recovery SR after an incorrect first prediction and posterior true-state
rank improvement after negative evidence. N4 reports Online Recovery SR,
replanning count, replanning delay, and excess path length relative to a
trajectory-aware oracle. This oracle is required because motion during execution
makes the conventional shortest path to a fixed terminal goal ill-defined.

For EQA, we report answer accuracy by existence, location, temporal, activity,
count, comparison, and reasoning question, together with category-macro accuracy.
Structured outputs are normalized to the benchmark ontology before scoring.

Benchmark aggregate results include bootstrap confidence intervals over episodes and
both micro and mobility-regime macro averages. We stratify results by paired
world variant, mobility regime, transition class (\emph{stay}, \emph{same-region
move}, \emph{cross-region move}, \emph{multi-move}, and \emph{return}),
staleness, spatial difficulty, grounding quality, and generalization split. The
key causal contrast is the paired routine gain: improvement in the routine world
relative to performance on its paired static and random variants. This contrast
tests whether gains arise from persistent temporal regularities rather than
scene geometry, identity, or generic search behavior. Implementation and
reporting details appear in \cref{app:implementation,app:protocol}.
